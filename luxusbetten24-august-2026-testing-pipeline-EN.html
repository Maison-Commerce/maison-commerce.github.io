Test 01 · Homepage · Roadmap HP-03

01 Add Review Volume and Three USPs Around the Hero CTA

Rating context and purchase reassurance at the point of decision

Primary: hero CTA click-through Min. runtime: 2 weeks Effort: low Confidence: high Scope: homepage

Source wireframe: homepage deck → Homepage Test 03

Problem

The hero already carries a Trustpilot badge above the headline, but it is stars and a wordmark only — no score, no review count. Stars alone don't communicate how much evidence sits behind them.

Reassurance is scattered elsewhere: free delivery rotates through the announcement bar, and the four real selling points sit in a “Why Luxusbetten24?” section seven sections below the hero buttons.

On mobile that leaves visitors deciding on the primary call to action with no support at all.

Solution

Keep the existing stars and add one line underneath, pulled from live Trustpilot data — the numerical score and the total review count. Make the stars and the line one clickable target to the Trustpilot profile.

Add a compact three-item reassurance row directly below the hero buttons: small icons, single-line labels, all three visible without a carousel, clear spacing from the buttons.

Leave the headline, sub-line, both button labels and their destinations untouched so the test isolates trust presentation.

Hypothesis

If the numerical rating and review count appear directly below the Trustpilot stars, and three clear selling points appear below the hero buttons, then more mobile visitors will click a primary call to action, because the store's social proof and purchase reassurance become legible at the moment of decision.

Evidence: live homepage audit — hero badge carries no score or volume, reassurance section sits seven sections lower; competitor comparison — Boxspring Welt and Mozart both surface numerical ratings and service reassurance; funnel data — 3.48% of sessions add to cart; homepage generated 37,635 landing sessions and grew 27%.

Test 02 · PDP · Roadmap PDP-01

02 Mobile Benefit Snapshot

Show the core benefits before the options

Primary: add-to-cart rate Min. runtime: 3 weeks Effort: low-medium Confidence: medium-high Scope: three highest-traffic PDPs

Source wireframe: PDP deck → PDP Test 01

Problem

The product title communicates the sleeping and folding function. Every other reason to buy — compact footprint, hidden storage, everyday versatility — is buried in the description.

The page proves the point twice over. There is a key-features checklist and a “4 reasons thousands fell in love” block, both written and both sitting below the add-to-cart button, the first of them inside a collapsed accordion.

Many visitors land directly on these pages and have seen no prior brand or product explanation. Between the price and the button they get eighteen colour swatches, a left-or-right choice and a single-option material selector — and no reason to want the sofa.

Solution

Add three concise, icon-supported benefit statements directly below the price and before the colour options:

  • Converts to a comfortable guest bed in seconds
  • Compact width — ideal for small rooms
  • Storage for blankets and cushions

Leave the price, financing message, all variant controls and the button unchanged so the test isolates early benefit communication.

All three statements are verifiable from copy already published lower on the same page — this promotes existing content rather than making new claims.

Hypothesis

If three product-specific benefits are displayed directly below the price and before the colour options, then more mobile visitors will begin product selection and add to cart, because they will understand the product's main value without scrolling into the long description.

Evidence: live PDP audit — benefits exist in the key-features list and the “4 reasons” block, both below the button; the reference PDP received 16,772 landing sessions in 30 days; sitewide add-to-cart rate is 3.48%; CRO practice for direct-entry, high-consideration product pages.

Test 03 · PDP · Roadmap PDP-02

03 Product Video as Gallery Item Two

Move the video ahead of the technical dimensions drawing

Primary: video play rate Min. runtime: 2 weeks Effort: very low Confidence: medium Scope: sitewide, all PDPs with video

Source wireframe: PDP deck → PDP Test 02

Problem

The video is not buried at the back of the gallery — it is already the third item. The problem is what sits in front of it: position two is a technical dimensions drawing, a measured line diagram rather than a product shot.

So the second thing a visitor swipes to, immediately after the lifestyle hero, is a spec drawing. The one asset that demonstrates the sleeping, folding and storage functions in motion is one swipe further back.

The gallery is also very large — roughly ninety items, eighteen colours photographed five ways each. Anything outside the first few positions is effectively invisible on mobile.

Solution

Move the existing product video to the second gallery position, directly after the main lifestyle image. The dimensions drawing takes position three.

Keep the first image, the video file, the thumbnail treatment, every remaining asset and all swipe behaviour unchanged, so the test isolates media order.

Nothing is added and nothing is removed — this is a swap of two adjacent slots.

Hypothesis

If the existing product video moves to the second position in the mobile gallery, then more visitors will watch it and add the product to cart, because its functionality will be demonstrated earlier in the decision journey.

Evidence: live gallery audit — order runs lifestyle, dimensions drawing, video, with roughly ninety items in total; product positioning — sleeping, folding and storage are the primary benefits; CRO practice: functional products benefit from demonstration rather than description alone.

Test 04 · PDP · Roadmap PDP-03a

04 Fabric Close-Up on Colour Selection

Make the chosen colour evaluable — all eighteen swatches stay in place

Primary: completed colour selection Min. runtime: 2 weeks Effort: medium Confidence: medium-high Scope: sitewide, excluding the three PDPs in test 02

Source wireframe: PDP deck → PDP Test 03a

Problem

At nineteen pixels, a swatch cannot carry the information a fabric decision needs. Sage against olive, blush against rosé, light grey against grey — on a phone these are the same dot. Corduroy has a visible rib, and none of that is legible at swatch size.

Nothing confirms the choice either. Tapping a swatch updates the label, but there is no enlarged view of the fabric the buyer is committing several hundred euros to.

Free fabric samples are offered, and the link sits right there — but as small text on the same line as the colour label, competing with the controls instead of supporting them, and placed before the doubt occurs rather than after.

Solution

Keep all eighteen swatches exactly as they are. When a colour is selected:

  • Show a large fabric close-up revealing the corduroy texture
  • Keep the colour name visible beside it
  • Update the main product image to the selected colour
  • Show a reassurance prompt after the choice offering a free fabric sample

Do not change which colours are available, how many are shown, or their pricing — every variant is the same price today, so there is no pricing complication.

Hypothesis

If the selected colour is shown as a large fabric close-up with its name, the main image updates to match, and the sample offer is promoted into its own prompt after the choice, then more visitors will complete colour selection and fewer will commit to a colour they later reject, because they can finally see what they are choosing.

Evidence: live PDP audit — eighteen swatches at nineteen pixels, no enlarged preview, sample link set as small competing text, all variants priced identically; existing free fabric-sample offer; sales reversals run at roughly 13.2% of gross sales; CRO practice for tactile and colour-sensitive furniture purchases.

Test 05 · Collection · Roadmap C-05

05 Sticky Top Filter and Sort Controls

Make filter and sort visible throughout browsing

Primary: filter-button click-through Min. runtime: 2 weeks Effort: low-medium Confidence: high Backed by a measured interaction signal

Source wireframe: collection deck → Collection Test 05

Problem

The combined filter-and-sort button is fixed to the bottom of the mobile viewport, where it competes with browser chrome, the messaging widget and other fixed elements.

Heatmap analysis by the CRO Manager shows it is the least-noticed control on the page by a wide margin — consistent with poor discoverability, though click share alone cannot separate visitors who missed it from visitors who did not need it.

With 157 products across eight pages, refinement is the main path to a suitable bed, and the control for it is the element people are least likely to see.

Solution

Remove the bottom control in the variant. Place two equal-width buttons directly below the collection title — filters and sort — and make the row sticky beneath the site header while the visitor scrolls.

Show the number of active filters dynamically in a badge. Keep the row compact so it does not obstruct product imagery, and add a subtle shadow when it enters its sticky state.

Preserve the existing drawers, their options and their logic. Make sure the messaging widget does not overlap either control, and remove the old bottom button so the variant tests placement rather than duplication.

Hypothesis

If separate filter and sort controls move above the product grid and stay visible while visitors scroll, then filter engagement, successful refinement and qualified product-page visits will increase, because the controls become easier to discover and use during browsing.

Evidence: CRO Manager heatmap analysis — the current filter button attracts a very small share of recorded clicks; live audit — filter and sort share one bottom control; collection structure — 157 products make refinement essential; the collection grew 61% and generated 11,957 landing sessions.

Test 06 · Collection · Roadmap C-03

06 Delivery and Feature Signals on Product Cards

Add decision support to product cards

Primary: collection-to-product click-through Min. runtime: 4 weeks Effort: medium Confidence: medium Needs delivery-time data populated

Source wireframe: collection deck → Collection Test 03

Problem

Cards show an image, a discount badge, title, review count, sale price with the original struck through, four colour swatches with an overflow count, and a view button. Delivery speed and major functions are absent.

The consequence is visible in the default results themselves. The first five positions are five near-identical beds — four of them variants of the same model, four sharing the same review count, all of them with storage, differing mainly in fabric and price. Nothing on the card explains which one to open.

Sales reversals run at roughly 13.2% of gross sales, so poorly informed clicks carry a real cost further down the funnel.

Solution

Add a maximum of two dynamic, text-based signals below the price:

  • A delivery message, for example a working-day lead time
  • The single strongest verified feature for that product — storage type, firmness range, or integrated lighting

Use only accurate product data and cap the module at two lines so cards stay scannable. Keep price, discount, review and colour presentation unchanged.

Hypothesis

If each product card displays delivery timing and one differentiating feature, then more collection visitors will click suitable products, because they can compare meaningful purchase criteria before opening multiple product pages.

Evidence: live card audit — no delivery or functional information present, and the first five default results are near-duplicate variants; competitor comparison — Betten.de surfaces delivery visibility; reversal data suggests better pre-click information may improve purchase qualification.

Test 07 · Cart · Roadmap Cart-02

07 Checkout-First Simplified Cart

Remove pre-checkout distractions

Primary: cart-to-checkout rate Min. runtime: 2 weeks Effort: medium Confidence: medium-high Decide on revenue per cart session

Source wireframe: cart deck → Cart Test 02

Problem

Before a visitor reaches the order summary and the lower checkout options, the page presents an app-install banner, a full navigation header, a rotating promotional strip, a chat banner, the upper checkout button, the product details and a cross-sell carousel.

Heatmap analysis by the CRO Manager shows real interaction on all four of those pre-checkout layers, and together they attract more clicks than the checkout button itself. The cross-sell module occupies substantial vertical space before the order summary while attracting almost none.

Those numbers show competing interaction. They do not prove that every click is harmful.

Solution

Build a checkout-focused variant:

  • Remove the app-install banner from the cart
  • Replace the full navigation header with the logo and a secure-cart label
  • Remove the wishlist and redundant cart-navigation icons
  • Remove the rotating promotional strip
  • Remove the large chat banner and retain a small support link below the primary checkout area
  • Move the cross-sell module below the standard checkout, express payments and purchase reassurance

Keep the cart item, price, discount, delivery information, payment methods and all underlying offers unchanged.

Hypothesis

If non-essential promotional, navigation, support and cross-sell elements are removed or moved below the checkout actions, then more cart visitors will proceed to checkout, because the page will contain fewer competing exits and decisions.

Evidence: CRO Manager cart audit — multiple promotional and navigation layers appear before core cart content; heatmap — measurable interaction on the app banner, navigation region, promotional strip and chat banner, with very little on cross-sell; funnel data — roughly 68% of add-to-cart sessions progress to checkout.

  4. Cross-sell moves below the standard checkout, express payments and reassurance. Every product and price is kept; it simply stops standing between the cart and the total.

What this test can and cannot prove

Do not optimise checkout clicks in isolation. Cart-to-checkout will very likely rise when four competing targets are deleted — that is close to arithmetic. Revenue per cart session is the metric that decides it.

Cross-sell attachment will probably fall, and those clicks carry order value. If attachment drops more than checkout rate gains, the change loses money. Track both.

App installs belong to someone else's target. The cart is a real acquisition surface for whoever owns the app. Agree the trade internally before launch, not after the readout.

Five changes at once. A legitimate concept test, but if it wins you will not know which removal did the work. Consider a follow-up that restores only the promotional strip — the most-clicked of the removed elements and the one most likely to be doing real promotional work.

Localisation note for the German store: the live cart renders the delivery-time string in English. Correct it in both arms before launch, or the test measures translation quality as well as layout.
